    How a soft belt finds a baby's heartbeat

    The NeoTex belt writes 100 numbers every second. Somewhere in those numbers are heartbeats, mixed with kicks, wiggles and loose contact. This page walks through every step we use to find the beats, compute the heart rate, and decide whether that heart rate can be trusted. Every plot is computed live, in your browser, with the same algorithm used in the paper.

    01Heartbeats, R-peaks and heart rate

    Each time the heart pumps, an electrical wave spreads across it. Electrodes on the skin pick up that wave as the ECG. The tallest, sharpest spike in each beat is the R-peak (part of the "QRS complex"). If we know when each R-peak happens, we know the heart rate.

    \[ \text{RR}_k = t_{k} - t_{k-1}\ \text{(seconds)} \qquad \text{HR} = \frac{60}{\text{RR}}\ \text{(beats per minute)} \]
    The time between two R-peaks is the RR interval.
    Easy exampleTwo beats 0.43 s apart: HR = 60 / 0.43 = 139.5 bpm. An adult at rest is around 60–100 bpm; a newborn is usually 120–160 bpm, so beats are only about 0.4 s apart. That is why infant ECG is harder: beats are close together and the QRS is narrow (about 50–60 ms).

    Sampling: 100 numbers per second

    The belt does not record a continuous curve. It writes one number every 10 ms. We call the n-th number \(x[n]\); its time is \(t = n / f_s\) with \(f_s = 100\) Hz. A narrow QRS of 50 ms is only about 5 samples wide, which is why every millisecond in the method matters.

    সহজ কথায়দুটো R-peak এর মাঝের সময় (RR) দিয়ে ৬০ ভাগ করলেই heart rate। শিশুর হার্ট দ্রুত চলে, তাই বিটগুলো খুব কাছাকাছি।

    02Step 1 · What the belt stores, \(x[n]\)

    The belt's chip (MAX30001) and firmware already do two things before saving. First, they remove the slow baseline by subtracting a 20-sample moving average. Second, they save the change from one sample to the next (a first difference). So the stored belt "ECG" does not look like a textbook ECG; each beat appears as a sharp up-down wiggle.

    \[ e[n] = \text{ECG}[n] - \frac{1}{20}\sum_{k=0}^{19}\text{ECG}[n-k], \qquad x[n] = e[n]-e[n-1] \]
    Firmware processing. Units: mV, 100 samples per second.
    x[n], stored belt ECGBIOPAC ECG, for comparison (scaled)
    Easy exampleThink of a hiker's altitude log. If you only write down "went up 3 m, went down 1 m, went up 5 m…", the steepest climbs stand out, and slow hills almost vanish. The belt stores ECG the same way, so slow drift from breathing is mostly gone, but sharp jolts from movement are still there.
    সহজ কথায়বেল্ট ECG এর মূল মান না রেখে প্রতি ধাপে কতটা বদলাল সেটা রাখে। তাই ধীর ঢেউ চলে যায়, কিন্তু ধাক্কা বা নড়াচড়ার spike থেকে যায়।

    03Step 2 · Band-pass filter, keep 5–25 Hz

    A band-pass filter keeps the speeds of change that belong to a QRS and removes the rest. Slow changes (below 5 Hz) are mostly body movement and breathing. Very fast changes (above 25 Hz) are mostly electrical noise. On this belt, the QRS energy sits between about 15 and 25 Hz, so we keep 5–25 Hz.

    \[ y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{4} b_k\,x[n-k] \;-\; \sum_{k=1}^{4} a_k\,y[n-k] \]
    A 2nd-order Butterworth band-pass design (4 poles). Each new output uses only the current and past inputs and past outputs, so it can run in real time ("causal"). The numbers \(b_k, a_k\) for the current band are listed below.

    04Step 3 · Differentiate, \(d[n]\)

    Next we take the difference between neighbouring samples again. A QRS is the steepest part of the ECG, so it produces the biggest differences. Gentle waves produce small ones.

    \[ d[n] = y[n] - y[n-1] \]
    Easy exampleYour car's speedometer. Position changes slowly while you cruise, but when you slam the brakes the speed changes fast. Differencing turns "where" into "how fast", and a heartbeat is the fastest thing in this signal.

    05Step 4 · Square, \(z[n]\)

    We square every value. Two things happen: negative values become positive (it no longer matters which way the electrodes face), and big values become much bigger than small ones.

    \[ z[n] = d[n]^2 \]
    Easy example0.2 becomes 0.04, 1 stays 1, and 3 becomes 9. Something 15 times bigger than the noise becomes 225 times bigger after squaring. Loud knocks now drown out the chatter.
    d[n] (differentiated)z[n] (squared)

    06Step 5 · Integrate, \(m[n]\)

    A single QRS can produce two or three separate spikes in \(z[n]\). We smooth them into one hump by averaging the last 5 samples (50 ms, about the width of an infant QRS). It only looks backwards, so it still works in real time.

    \[ m[n] = \frac{1}{W}\sum_{k=0}^{W-1} z[n-k], \qquad W = 5 \text{ samples} = 50\text{ ms} \]
    z[n]m[n], one hump per beat
    Easy exampleA knock is "knock-knock-knock" in quick succession, but you count it as one visitor. Averaging over a short window joins the pieces of one beat into one hump.

    07Step 6 · Decide which humps are beats

    Now we need a line: humps above it are beats, humps below it are noise. A fixed line would fail, because the signal gets bigger and smaller as the baby moves or the belt shifts. So the detector keeps two running memories and puts the line between them.

    S · signal level

    A running estimate of how tall real beats are.

    N · noise level

    A running estimate of how tall the rejected bumps are.

    THR · the line

    A quarter of the way from N up to S. Anything above it is a beat.

    \[ \text{THR} = N + \beta\,(S - N), \qquad \beta = 0.25 \] \[ \text{beat accepted:}\; S \leftarrow \lambda\,\min(m,\,4S) + (1-\lambda)\,S \qquad \text{bump rejected:}\; N \leftarrow \lambda\,m + (1-\lambda)\,N, \qquad \lambda = 0.125 \]
    Start values (first 2 s): \(S = 0.5\max m\), \(N = \text{mean}(m)\). \(\lambda = 1/8\) means each new peak moves the memory one-eighth of the way.

    Three safety rules

    200 ms refractory

    After a beat, ignore everything for 200 ms. A heart cannot beat again that fast (it would be 300 bpm), so a second hump that soon is the T-wave or noise.

    Search-back

    If no beat has come for 1.66 × the average of the last 8 RR intervals, we probably missed one. Look back and accept the biggest hump if it is above half the line.

    Guards (v2)

    Clip: one huge motion spike may raise S by at most 4×, so it cannot push the line out of reach. Decay: if no beat for 2 s, S slowly falls toward N (time constant 1 s), so the detector cannot get stuck.

    Easy exampleA teacher deciding who is "shouting" in a class. She remembers how loud shouting usually is (S) and how loud background talk usually is (N), and calls anything a quarter of the way above the background a shout. If the whole class gets louder, both memories rise and the line moves with them. Try β = 0.6 on the "Big kick" clip and watch beats go missing; try β = 0.05 and watch noise become "beats".
    সহজ কথায়দুটো স্মৃতি রাখি: আসল বিট কত বড় (S) আর noise কত বড় (N)। লাইনটা N থেকে S এর দিকে এক-চতুর্থাংশ উপরে। সিগন্যাল বড়-ছোট হলে লাইনও সাথে সাথে সরে।

    07bDetector v1 and v2: the two guards

    The belt detector exists in two versions. They share every step on this page and the same settings (5–25 Hz, 50 ms, β = 0.25, 200-ms refractory, search-back). The only difference is how the signal level \(S\) is allowed to change. v1 came first. v2 added two guards after we found that v1 could go silent for the rest of a recording. v2 is the version used in the paper.

    Belt v1

    Every accepted beat pulls S one-eighth of the way toward its height. Nothing else ever changes S.

    S ← λ·m + (1 − λ)·S

    Belt v2 · guard 1, clip

    One beat may raise S to at most 4 times its current value, however tall the spike is.

    S ← λ·min(m, 4S) + (1 − λ)·S

    Belt v2 · guard 2, decay

    If no beat is accepted for 2 s, S slides back toward N with a time constant of 1 s.

    S ← N + (S − N)·e−1/(τ·fs), τ = 1 s

    \[ \textbf{v1:}\; S \leftarrow \lambda\,m + (1-\lambda)\,S \qquad\qquad \textbf{v2:}\; S \leftarrow \lambda\,\min(m,\,4S) + (1-\lambda)\,S,\quad S \xrightarrow{\;\text{no beat for 2 s}\;} N + (S-N)\,e^{-t/\tau} \]
    λ = 0.125, fs = 100 Hz, τ = 1 s. The decay is applied one sample at a time, so after 2 s without a beat the gap S − N shrinks by about 63 % per second.

    Why v1 can go silent

    The integrated energy \(m[n]\) is a squared quantity. A movement spike 20 times taller than a normal beat produces about 400 times more energy. In v1, that one spike drags S far upward, the line THR = N + 0.25(S − N) ends up above every real beat, and S can only come down when a beat is accepted, which now never happens. The detector is locked until the end of the recording. v2 prevents both halves of this: the clip limits how far S can jump, and the decay brings it back if the beats stop.

    Easy exampleOur teacher from step 6 hears a fire alarm and decides "that is how loud shouting is". From then on no child's shout is loud enough, and because she only updates her idea of "loud" when she hears a shout, she never recovers (v1). In v2 she is not allowed to raise her idea of "loud" more than 4× at once, and if two seconds pass without any shout she starts to forget the alarm.

    08Step 7 · Put the beat at the right moment

    The hump in \(m[n]\) arrives a little after the real R-peak, because the filter and the 5-sample average both add delay. So for each accepted hump we look back 120 ms and ahead 20 ms in the band-passed signal \(y[n]\), and take the sample with the largest size (either sign).

    \[ r = \arg\max_{\,n\, \in\, [\,p-12,\; p+2\,)} \big|\,y[n]\,\big| \]
    \(p\) is the hump position; 12 samples = 120 ms before, 2 samples = 20 ms after.
    y[n]m[n] (scaled)hump peak prefined R time rshaded: search window
    Easy exampleYou hear thunder a moment after the lightning. If you want the time of the lightning, you look back a little from when you heard the thunder.

    09Step 8 · Heart rate every 10 seconds

    We cut the recording into 10-second blocks that do not overlap. Inside each block we list the RR intervals and use the median, the middle value, not the average. A block needs at least 4 beats.

    \[ \text{HR}_{\text{window}} = \frac{60}{\operatorname{median}(\text{RR}_1,\dots,\text{RR}_K)} \]
    Easy exampleRR = 0.43, 0.43, 0.86, 0.43 s, where one beat was missed so one interval is doubled. Average = 0.54 s, giving 112 bpm (wrong). Median = 0.43 s, giving 140 bpm (right). The median ignores one bad interval.
    সহজ কথায়প্রতি ১০ সেকেন্ডে RR গুলোর median নিই, গড় না। একটা বিট miss হলেও median প্রায় বদলায় না।

    10Step 9 · Can we trust this number?

    This is the most important step. Every 10-second block must pass three checks. If any one fails, the belt withholds the heart rate for that block rather than showing a wrong number.

    1 · Plausible

    \(80 \le \text{HR} \le 220\) bpm. Outside this, it is almost certainly a detection error for an infant.

    2 · Steady

    \(\text{CV} = \dfrac{\text{SD(RR)}}{\text{mean(RR)}} < 0.25\). Missed or extra beats make the intervals jumpy.

    3 · Clear

    Run the detector a second time, stricter (\(\beta = 0.5\), no search-back). If both runs find the same beats, the beats were obvious. \(F_1 \ge 0.90\).

    \[ F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{TP}}{n_1 + n_2} \]
    \(n_1\), \(n_2\): beats found by the normal and the strict run in this block. TP: beats of the normal run that have a strict-run beat within ±75 ms.
    Easy exampleTwo people count knocks independently, one of them only counting the loud ones. Normal count 25, strict count 24, 24 match. F1 = 2·24 / (25+24) = 0.98: they agree, so trust it. If the strict person only heard 15 of 25, F1 = 30/40 = 0.75: the knocks were not clear, so do not trust the count.

    11Checking against the wired reference

    To know if the belt was right, we compare its beats with BIOPAC beats. A belt beat with a BIOPAC beat within ±75 ms is a true positive (TP). A belt beat with no partner is a false positive (FP, an extra beat). A BIOPAC beat with no belt partner is a false negative (FN, a missed beat).

    \[ \text{Sensitivity} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FN}} \qquad \text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}} \]
    TP✕ FP (extra)○ FN (missed)grey ✕ ○ unverified: BIOPAC failed its own check thereBIOPAC

    The reference can fail too. On "Pulling the wire" the baby tugs the BIOPAC lead and, in the first block, BIOPAC fails its own quality check. A belt beat with no BIOPAC partner there is not proof of a belt error: BIOPAC may have missed it, and BIOPAC may also have produced beats of its own from the noise. So in any block where BIOPAC fails its own check, unmatched beats are shown in grey as unverified and left out of sensitivity and precision. Only blocks where the reference passed its own check are scored. Whether the belt was right in the grey blocks is judged by a person looking at both traces (or by the belt's own trust check), never by assuming either device wins.

    The movement sensor and the video say why a block is hard (a kick, a hand on the wire). They are not used to decide which device is correct, because that would let the explanation decide the score.

    13Where the settings came from

    None of the numbers (5–25 Hz, 50 ms, β = 0.25, the guards) were picked by hand to make the belt look good. They were chosen offline, once, and then frozen.

    Expert labels

    A reviewer checked 1,819 eight-second strips (48 sessions, 10 infants, about 21,900 beats), adding, deleting or moving beat marks and grading each strip's quality.

    Choose on half, test on the other half

    Band, β and window were chosen on odd-numbered labelled strips and tested on even ones. The guards were chosen on odd-numbered infants and checked on even-numbered infants.

    Then frozen

    The same settings run on every recording. Nobody corrects beats at run time.

    Context, not part of the decision

    The belt's motion sensor (IMU), a 60-second contact check at the start of a session, and the annotated video are used only to explain failures afterwards (was the baby moving? was the belt loose?). They do not change which beats are found or whether a heart rate is reported.

    Easy exampleA teacher writes the exam before seeing the students' answers. If you tune the rules after seeing the answers, the score means nothing.

    14Explain it to someone else

    Read this aloud. It is the whole method in six sentences.

    1. The belt records the baby's heart signal 100 times a second, already stored as "how much it changed".
    2. We keep only the 5–25 Hz part, where heartbeats live, and remove slow movement and fast noise.
    3. We exaggerate the sharp parts (difference, square) and join each beat into one hump (50 ms average).
    4. A self-adjusting line, a quarter of the way from the noise level to the beat level, decides which humps are beats, with three safety rules.
    5. Every 10 seconds we compute heart rate from the median gap between beats.
    6. We only show that number if it is plausible, steady, and found again by a stricter second detector; otherwise we honestly say "not reliable".

    Check yourself

    Why do we square the signal?

    To make every value positive (electrode direction does not matter) and to make big peaks much bigger than small noise.

    Why the median RR and not the mean?

    One missed beat doubles one interval. The mean is pulled far off; the median barely moves.

    Why does the threshold move?

    Signal size changes with movement and contact. A line that follows the recent beat and noise levels keeps working when the signal gets bigger or smaller.

    What does "withhold" mean, and why is it good?

    The block failed a check, so no heart rate is shown for it. A monitor that says "I am not sure" is safer than one that shows a confident wrong number.

    Is F1 ≥ 0.90 the same as "90% accurate"?

    No. It means two runs of the detector agree on about 90% of beats. Accuracy is measured separately against BIOPAC.

    Why is the R time searched 120 ms back but only 20 ms forward?

    The filter and the averaging window delay the hump, so the real R-peak is almost always before the hump.

    Glossary

    ECG
    Electrocardiogram: the heart's electrical signal measured on the skin.
    R-peak, QRS
    The sharp spike of each beat; the QRS complex is the whole spike (Q dip, R peak, S dip).
    Sample, fs
    One stored number; fs is how many per second (100 Hz for the belt).
    Hz
    Cycles per second. Low Hz = slow changes, high Hz = fast changes.
    Band-pass
    A filter that keeps only a range of frequencies.
    Causal
    Uses only the present and the past, so it can run live.
    RR interval
    Time between two consecutive R-peaks.
    CV
    Coefficient of variation: standard deviation divided by mean; how jumpy the RR intervals are.
    TP / FP / FN
    Correct beat / extra beat / missed beat, compared with a reference.
    F1
    Agreement score between two sets of beats, 0 to 1.
    β, λ
    β places the line between noise and signal; λ is how fast the memories S and N update.
    BIOPAC
    The wired laboratory system used as the reference.
